## Fiction

“The Nights Are Quiet in Tehran” was listed for the International Booker award, and then it was selected for book club. Spanning four decades and five members of an Iranian family, we start with the 1979 revolution. A communist revolutionary has hopes for his country, only to see them dashed when religious zealots wrest power. Tens years later, the family is in exile in Germany. Each section is a different family member, a decade apart. I appreciated that we start with a man participating positively in the revolution, since most fictional tales I’ve read of the time focus more on unexpected victimhood. The story of displacement explores a lot of nuance, and it is a bit more interesting for coming out in English nearly a decade after its German publication.

I first heard of “Angel Down” when it won the Pulitzer. In the waning days of WWI, Private Bagger has been avoiding combat so far by drawing upon his skills as a con man. Then he and four other soldiers are sent into No Man’s Land to deal with a screaming voice, only to find that it is an angel trapped in barbed wire. I listened to the audiobook, but the frenetic nature comes across well, since this book is a single unbroken sentence. The novel used this to good effect near the end. I did find the story interesting, though I did struggle with the reactions of the different characters. Only recommended for fans of literary fiction, I don’t find it a compelling addition to WWI narratives.

## Short Fiction

I picked up “A Plagued Sea” because Kim Bo-young is on my watchlist after her excellent collection, “On the Origin of Species and Other Stories”. Mu-young works as a bodyguard, but is on a train with her niece when a massive earthquake hits the village of Haewon. Three years later, the village is quarantined, and many residents have “East Sea” disease. Mu-young works as a deputy, enforcing quarantine orders, but things are disrupted when a man arrives from the capital looking to research current conditions. I went in blind, not realizing this story is a reference to another work, and I much enjoyed the reveal about halfway through. It’s a good novella, pulling in themes about the role of women and other social questions. Worth checking out for cosmic horror fans.

I quite enjoyed the last Thomas Ha story I read, so picking up “Our Revels Now Are Ended” was a no-brainer. Awakened in a library, a man with redacted memories is questioned by a state interrogator, Saul. Surely his memories were tampered with for a reason, but the interrogator doesn’t know if its because of guilt or association. And, there are other interrogations going on at the same time. I found the concept here clever, and the structure reveals itself quite well. I haven’t read/seen “The Tempest” recently enough to catch the title reference. I’ll definitely be reading more of this author’s work.
