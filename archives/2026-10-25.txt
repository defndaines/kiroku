## Fiction

I’ve been meaning to read more Toni Morrison, so was happy when “Song of Solomon” was selected for book club. Milkman’s birth and the book starts with a man taking flight from a rooftop. Set primarily in the ’50s and ’60s, we follow Milkman, raised in a Michigan town by a father focused almost entirely on property ownership and a mother whose most known for being the daughter of the former doctor, the most prominent Black man in town for his generation. As the son of perhaps the richest Black man in town, he is isolated and self-absorbed. His father has broken ties with Milkman’s aunt, but our protagonists seeks to learn more. This book is an unexpected delight, as we wade through years in the life of a man I had little concern or sympathy for. But Morrison didn’t win the Nobel for nothing, her craft is impeccable, both in the beautiful and poignant writing and composing a complex story that pulls on all sorts of threads. I need to read more of her work next year.

I requested “Autobiography of Red” after Anne Carson was awarded the 2026 Nobel. A novel in verse, we follow the childhood and early adulthood of Geryon, a boy with red wings. The story is an interpretation of the work of the Greek poet Stesichoros. Geryon was murdered by Herakles in his tenth labor in the traditional story, but here Herakles is Geryon’s first real friend and more. I can see the artistry of the writing, but from this text I would describe Carson as a writer’s writer. I wasn’t clear on several plot points until I read an external summary, and I was not drawn into the tale in any way. The best I can say is that I have read another Nobel Prize winner, as this might be my most “meh” book of the year.

I wanted to reread “The Fifth Season” at some point, and book club selected the series to finish out the year. The story weaves three different tales. We have Essun, a middle-aged woman who must set off to find her daughter after a major earthquake has begun a “fifth season”. We have Damaya, a young girl who is rejected by her family when her powers are exposed, then travels to the Fulcrum to be trained in service of the state. And we have Syenite, a Fulcrum graduate on the cusp of reaching a power level that brings a bit of freedom, but first she has to endure a mentor who grates on her. This book is a masterpiece of world-building, bringing in details slowly that reward again on a second read. It also explores real-world social issues in a way that many fantasy novels strive for, but few attain. A must-read for everyone, and a reminder to me that I need to got to her Inheritance trilogy next year.

## Short Fiction

I grabbed “Commensalism, Or the Labyrinth’s Vessels” after it won the 2026 Ignyte Award. Forty-two, a cook from Lagos, wakes up from statis to find humanity controlled by an entity called the Labyrinth. The Labyrinth has been trying to understand why the human under its control keep dying, and Forty-two is, well, the 42nd human it has enlisted to try to understand the conundrum that is humanity. I had to look it up, but on the spectrum of symbiotic to parasitic, commensalism is the middle ground where you do no harm. I liked the premise of the story and appreciated how it unfurled. I’ve only read one of the other short story nominees for this year, but I would give that story an edge over this one. Still worth reading.

## Nonfiction

I owned the first edition of “Designing Data-Intensive Applications”, but never read it cover to cover. We worked through the second edition over five months as part of a book club at work. This is one of the must-reads for software engineers, and it shows up on pretty much every recommendation list out there, and for good reason. The authors walk us systematically through the different parts of an application, with what can go wrong and what tactics there are to try to address it when things do in fact go wrong. Even if you work in a complex distributed system already, there’s something to learn here. And the last chapter is perhaps the best, putting the perfect bow on.
