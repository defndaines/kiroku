## Fiction

Having recently re-read “The Iliad”, I wanted to queue up a related retelling. In “The Song of Achilles”, we follow the life of Patroclus, exiled prince fostered in the house of King Peleus. He forms a deep bond with Peleus’ son, Achilles. They train under Chiron, but the fate of his demigod friend cannot be avoided, no matter how many years Achilles hides as a woman on Skyros. We know the fate that awaits both of them in the Trojan war, but this take on it adds deeper context and color. It wasn’t perfect, but definitely worth the read.

I started this series when the first book was nominated for (and won) the Hugo Award last year. “A Trade of Blood” continues Din and Ana’s detective work in the empire. This time, a young gentry woman has been killed by the son of a rival gentry clan. In their district, the two families dominate the meat industry. You know there’s more to the story. Another excellent addition to the series which I would recommend to anyone (as indicated by my having read it within two weeks of its release).

## Short Fiction

In “Whale Fall”, a sky whale crashes into a near-future New York City. Dar and Porter, roommates and sometimes more, rush across the city to reach the carcass, competing with other scavengers to harvest parts of the carcass before the cops arrive. Will their recent fight interfere with their opportunity to pay rent? This is a fun mashup of dystopian world building with coming to terms with one’s relationships.

I’d actually read “The Spindle of Necessity” before, probably when I first started digging into Ignyte Award nominees. Andrew, a trans man, is intimately drawn to the works of Samantha Finnes, an obscure mid-century lesbian author of novels of gay men. Andrew has a theory that Finnes was secretly trans. His fixation leads to lucid dreams where he encounters her face-to-face, but upon waking finds novels of hers with his notes in them but no memory of having read them. This is a meditation on identity with a mild hint of supernatural. I found the text so unmemorable that it took me a while to realize I’d read it before, and couldn’t even remember how it ended.

A reread for me, I read “A Stranger Knocks” because it was a nominee for both the Ignyte and Locus Awards. In 1926, a young, black couple in D.C. are house sitting for a professor when a stranger appears at the door. He seems to know details about them which he shouldn’t, but he also has a rather profitable offer: borrowing the professor’s car to escort him around the East Coast screening a silent film for black audiences. This short horror piece highlights a little known history of black cinema in an engaging tale. This is only my second piece of Due’s, and I like it well enough that I will seek out more though horror isn’t in my usual genres.

I picked up “We Who Will Not Die” because it won the 2025 Ignyte Award for Outstanding Novelette. We start with a letter set to be burned. We then move backward and forward in a timeline, learning the fate of Nima and her friend Tuni. On their remote planet, people remember their past lives, and they have developed a culture around reincarnation. But now the Wageni have arrived to “integrate” into the planet. This story is excellent and highly recommended. A couple quotes that highlight why I found it impactful. “… alliances as a word carries friend at the tip of its tongue and subjugation at the back.” “Their court convicts the criminalized, not the guilty.”

I came across “Parthenogenesis” because it was nominated for both the Ignyte and Locus Awards. Matty and Jac have been friends for a long time. Now they are moving across country when their budget moving truck breaks down. While waiting for the mechanic, they build a story about the strange statue they encounter, part bear, part wolf, and part elk. It’s not my favorite Stephen Graham Jones, but still an enjoyable short story.

I came across “The Future Library” because it won an Ignyte Award. We read the 2125 letter of Ingrid, a former arborist for the last forest on the planet, convicted of “eco-terrorism”. The Future Library tasked 100 authors with writing a story only to be published 100 years after their passing, printed using paper made from a single tree in the forest. Ingrid loved Claire, the woman in charge of the library and also a contributing author. But when they cut down the first tree in honor of Claire’s passing, there is a surprise waiting for everyone. A solid work of eco-fiction, there are some odd details that felt unrealistic, but I did enjoy the tale and message overall. Recommended.

I have read a couple of Premee Mohamed’s award-nominated works, so picked up “Everyone Keeps Saying Probably”. A father addresses his infant son about the world following a “warning shot” from space that has changed life for all humanity. He is the primary caregiver, while mom is an astrophysicist working on the problem. This is a meditation on what it means to raise a new generation in an apocalyptic scenario. I found the tale rather flat, but will give the author another try.

I forget how I came across “Tomorrow’s Beautiful Dream”, but a dystopian sci-fi tale with a working-class protagonist translated from Chinese is right up my alley. Meng Wan scrapes by as a “soulseller”, renting out his body via a neural implant that lets him be worked to exhaustion, only to apply healing salves and start all over again the next day. But then a new job opportunity arises with much better income. Is it too good to be true? An excellent story about the conditions of workers under capitalism, where there technology allows for the mind and body to be separated. Recommended.

“A Shaky Bridge” follows Casey as she deals with the aftermath not only of her father’s stroke, but the neural implant that has allowed him to return to a normal life. But then he starts repeating ads for the company and making statement sympathetic to the company’s CEO, flying in the face of his behavior before the stroke. How can she wade through the propaganda and astroturf online to figure out what’s really happening to her father. This is full “Black Mirror” vibes. If I hadn’t encountered the same rough outline before, the ideas may have had more of an impact. I did like the emphasis at the end, even if the overall story fell a little flat for me.

I’m not sure how I came across “Understudies”. Vince is growing up in the age of A.I. that allows some students to get ahead by having a constant neural companion. His family is struggling, but an uncle gifts him glasses that enhance his ability to learn by using scents. Vince begins to excel at school, but is convinced that there are some things a human should still be able to do better than a computer. This was a bit tedious to read, with a huge portion of the text taken up by mathematical and logic problems that the students are presented with, including their solutions. The first and latter parts felt a bit disconnected, and the thoughts about A.I. didn’t feel particularly novel.

I came across “Glass Bottle Dancer” because it won the World Fantasy Award. Mable is a Virgin Islands civil servant with several kids, the youngest sixteen. Then the idea of glass bottle dancing captures her, and she invents a new form of dancing, practicing at night while her husband is asleep. Meanwhile, the local cockroaches have been paying attention, mostly to avoid getting crushed in the efforts. This is a fun tale written in the Virgin Islands dialect. I wasn’t sure what to expect, since the “fantasy” label isn’t at all obvious until the cockroaches start talking to one another. I did like it, though, for all its silliness.

I picked up “The Birding” because it won the World Fantasy Award. Maria is pregnant and bringing her father along as she tries to return home to her husband. The only thing is, much of humanity has turned into birds, and the process is underway for her father. She encounters El, also struggling to survive, and ends up sharing the fairy tale she’s composing for her unborn child. A quirky tale about survival and family, I’m glad I picked it up. I have another book by the author in my queue to read at some point.

I’ve read a lot of Nghi Vo, but mostly the Singing Hills series, so I wanted to try something else by her. In “The City in Glass”, the demon Vitrine has spent centuries building up the city Azril. She keeps records of its people and history in a book stored within a glass cage in her heart. But then angels show up and destroy everything, leading Vitrine to wound one of the angels, binding them together through the aftermath of the destruction, clean-up, and rebuilding. I thought this was going to be a bit adventurous, but this is a slow tale focused on the world building. There are genuinely interesting ideas in here, but I’m not sure who I would recommend this to.

I picked up “Das Steingeschöpf” because it won the World Fantasy Award. It is 1928, and Hertzel has finished his apprenticeship as a Schöpfer. His first commission has him traveling to rural Bavaria to assist an elderly women in repairing her Steingeschöpf (you have to read the story to put together what that is). But this isn’t an ordinary piece, and working on one takes more than physical effort on the part of the craftsman. This is a well constructed tale, delving into the young man’s life and the growing antisemitism of ’20s Germany. I liked the way the mystery built, even if there wasn’t strong tension to the story.

## Nonfiction

“Plastic Inc.” was selected for book club, and I hadn’t heard of it previously. Oil and petrochemical companies, faced with a future of declining fuel demand, are somehow expanding. We trace back to the origins of plastic and how it has been an important engine of growth for oil companies for a long time. As they burn the earth down around us, they continue to lie to us about “recycling” garbage that seems to pollute everything it touches. All the while, blaming us for the changes they have heave upon us. The book does offer tiny rays of hope near the end. I definitely recommend this book, but be warned that it is rage inducing.

“The Destruction of Palestine Is the Destruction of the Earth” was selected for book club, and it is my second Malm read. A collection of essays by the author, he situates the devastation and genocide in Gaza against the long history of imperial subjugation in the name of fossil fuels. The centrality of oil to this situation is outlined, drawing connections to capitalism’s insatiable need to crush anyone underfoot if they dare suggest that humanity and the environment matter more than profits. He does a good job of breaking down some of the counter-arguments, such as the “lobbyist” theory that doesn’t hold water. I like Malm’s writing and would recommend this book.
