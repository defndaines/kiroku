## Fiction

This was my second read of “The Iliad”, but I don’t remember which translation I read over twenty years ago. In the final weeks of the decade-long Trojan War, Agamemnon provokes Achilles by wrongheadedly taking a beautiful slave woman from the son of goddess Thetis. For all the harm that Diomedes and the others deal to the Trojans, Hector, son of Priam, is a powerful foe who leads Trojans to nearly drive off the Greeks. So, Achilles accedes to Patroclus pushing them back. What can I say about a classic? The Wilson translation is excellent. One thing that stood out this read: the second most used weapon after a spear is a big rock.

I put a hold on “Teddy Bears Never Die” as soon as I saw the release announcement. Sixteen-year-old Hwayoung lost her mother during a mass poisoning three years ago, but she’s convinced that her mother didn’t die from the poisoning. She’s been working hard to raise money that she thinks will get her justice. And then she encounters a teddy bear possessed by the spirit of a former classmate whose parents also died in the poisoning. Something shady is going on in Yamu, and these two are going to figure it out. This is a fun, gonzo story that takes you unaware at times because you aren’t sure where reality holds versus the supernatural. But you can be sure that rich psychopaths are behind it all, just like in real life.

I picked up “Train Dreams” because it is on the NYT’s 100 of the 21st century list. Robert Grainer is a day laborer in the American northwest, working in lumber or on bridge building. We jump around in his life, from his childhood to his brief marriage, ending up as an old man who hasn’t taken to the new technology of the 1960s. The book is well written, but I didn’t find myself pulled into the story. I cannot quite wrap my head around why it has received the praise it has.

With “On the Calculation of Volume IV”, Solvej Balle becomes my most read author of the year so far. Midway through the series now, Tara is encamped in the house in Bremen as more and more people who are stuck in the 18 November loop learn of each other. The diverse experiences allows for a great sampling of information, if not understanding. A solid addition to the series with some headier discussions than I felt like III had. Now I have to wait until November for the next one to be translated.

## Nonfiction

The Civil War saw the abolition of slavery, but it was not made illegal until WWII. Blackmon lays out the ways that the wealthy of the South and their lackeys (sheriffs, judges, etc.) built up “vagrancy” and other laws to criminalize Black existence. They then used that to re-enslave the Black population via convict leasing and debt peonage. Mines and mills and other industry across the South was built up in the late 1800s on the back of enslaved labor. Absent accountability or responsibility, working conditions frequently lead to death. This is essential reading. It will make you angry. While showing the labor conditions the ruling class of today salivates over, we also see how thoroughly we will have to work to destroy white supremacy.

## Graphic Novels

I’d been meaning to read Bechdel for a while, so checked out “Fun Home” from the library. Allison grew up in a small town in northwestern Pennsylvania. Her parents were educated and even lived in Europe for a while, but Allison and her siblings grew up homebound. She explores the circumstances around her father’s death as well as her coming into her awareness as a lesbian. This is a literary text, her father being one of those rare people who read “À la Recherche du Temps Perdu”. Having finished this, I’m also inspired to read “Ulysses”. It’s a slow, dense read, but recommended if you have a taste for literary comics.

In “Synthetics”, it is 2074 and humanity has maybe fifteen more years to live. Unable to clean up the dead zones, many have isolated themselves at home or in voidspace, while rage gangs cause chaos in the streets. Scientists are able to regrow limbs for people and backup human memories, but it is illegal to try to recreate an entire human. Two Chicago cops are tracking down some illegal biohacking that leads them to the doorsteps of a top scientist. There were some potentially interesting ideas in here, but the storytelling felt too abbreviated. The juxtaposition of high tech with social collapse lacked verisimilitude for me. I didn’t care about what was going to happen, and only finished because it was short.
