## Fiction

I got a free copy of “The Hunger of Those Who Built It” at Worldcon. Set in near-future Paris, two sisters have different ideas about what it will take to get humanity through climate disaster. Diane architects a green wall around Paris so that the city might attain sustainability and food security. Helen is sure the effort will only benefit the select few. Helen’s daughter will see how it turned out in her lifetime. This book straddles dystopian and hope punk in a way I’ve not seen before. I found it a bit slow; a lot of ink spent on Diane can feel like it isn’t progressing. But the pacing helps drive home the message. Also, the title is an Ursula K. Le Guin reference which you might wait to look up until you’ve finished.

I decided to pick up “The Samurai and the Prisoner” when I heard about the movie based upon it. It is a mystery set during the 1578 siege of Arioka Castle. The lord of the castle, Araki Murashige, turns against Nobunaga. Kuroda Kanbei comes as a messenger from Nobunaga, and instead of letting him return alive or sending back his head, Murashige takes Kanbei prisoner, a most uncommon decision. As the siege draws on, strange incidents challenge the lord of the castle, each eventually compelling Murashige to seek counsel with his prisoner. This book is excellent. Much of the book feels like court intrigue, which I normally wouldn’t be interested in, resting on the deductions of subtle actions and statements that Murashige must parse.

## Short Fiction

“People Shoot People” begins with a death. Lexi’s late partner died protesting the system. Now she is struggling to pick up the pieces, living in a “smart” home where all the appliances talk, but don’t learn that Lexi now lives alone. Then a piece of technology shows up at her doorstep, infused with the uploaded personality of her late partner. This is quality sci-fi. It’s a bit confusing at first, and the second-person perspective might throw off some, but I can see this being an award contender.

After talking with Thomas Ha at Worldcon, I’m on a mission to read more of his work. In “The Sort”, a father and son are traveling and stop in a small town. The father is protective of his neurodivergent son, but also knows the boy needs to experience the world more. Some of their encounters are pleasant, but there is the ever-present risk that things can quickly turn hostile. This story is highly recommended, a must-read for sci-fi readers.

“To Walk this Distant Shore with You” begins with two children watching rockets set off for a distant colony world, one of them declaring that they will go there some day. But colonization involves a lot of hard work, and being a pilot means having your consciousness spread across light years. There are some interesting concepts in this story, but I have to admit it didn’t quite grab me.

In “Scion”, the narrator is the heir to the Levinlord—who operates a large, ever-shifting manse in isolation. Knowing his father’s health is failing, the narrator sets off to find his father. He is aided by an A.I., his relatives, and the su-folk who are the servants of the house. Mostly raised apart from his father, he isn’t sure that he’ll be able to take on the job of creating the storms that protect their moon from the forces that sought to control them generations before. This novella was slow and confusing for me, and as I pieced it together, I wasn’t drawn into the characters or world. There are interesting ideas here, but it is my least favorite of Ha’s stories that I’ve read so far.

I am interested in reading Wen Wen Yang’s short stories after seeing her on a couple panels at Worldcon. In “That Summer at Aunt Jackie’s Alpaca Farm”, JingJing is left in charge of the farm while her aunt is away. The alpaca’s wool has healing properties, and those will come in handy when the highest-ranked male of the flock comes into conflict with Liam, the son of a beekeeping family. This is a fun fantasy story, quite short, that reads as a meet-cute as well. I’ll definitely be reading more of the author’s work.

Set during the gold rush, “Lu’s Laundry and Other Services” follows Lu, who has already been in America for fifteen years. The ghost of a prospector shows up at her laundry and demands vengeance, something a discriminated-against member of society is in no position to grant. But when she finds out who the perpetrator of the murder is, she might just feel compelled to help. The story is over in no time, but it is one of the best short stories I’ve read this year. I hope to see it nominated for some awards.

I really had trouble following “And So I Open My Fists”. The point of the story is to play around with gender and sexual ideas, with a world where women impregnate men, who can eventually turn into women. The characters are dealing with their emotions from having been in a relationship with someone who has now transitioned. It all ended up just being muddled for me. With no strong plot or characters that pulled me into the world, it felt like a concept waiting for a story.

## Nonfiction

“Racist by Design” is my second book by Kelly Lytle Hernández. The author breaks down the history of U.S. immigration policy, showing from the very start it was rooted in racism, whether against black migrants or Chinese. At the same time, the first acceptance of refugees was white refugees fleeing a majority black nation. We explore the legal shifts and turns, and we see the openly stated motivations of the people who wrote the laws and court decisions that got us where we are today. We see the way the U.S. pioneered a new vision of what it meant to be a citizen. This is an excellent and detailed overview of immigration policy. Although I’ve read more than a few books on the topic, there was still more to learn. And, I came away with an even stronger conviction that we should do away with all borders, there is no redeeming a system flawed at its core.
